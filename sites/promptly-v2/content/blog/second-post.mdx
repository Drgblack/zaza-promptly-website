# Feedback that lands: Making comments students actually read

You've spent precious time crafting thoughtful comments, only to watch students glance at the grade and file the work away. Sound familiar? The problem isn't that students don't care—it's often that our feedback doesn't connect with how they learn best.

## The feedback disconnect

Research consistently shows that students skip over comments when they:
- Feel too overwhelming or generic
- Don't clearly connect to their actual work
- Lack specific guidance on improvement
- Come without opportunity to act on the advice

The good news? Small changes to how we frame and deliver feedback can dramatically increase student engagement.

## Start with what works

Before addressing areas for growth, anchor your feedback in genuine recognition of student efforts and achievements. This isn't about false praise—it's about helping students understand their own learning process.

**Instead of**: "Good work on this essay."  
**Try**: "Your opening paragraph immediately draws the reader in by connecting the theme to a contemporary issue—this shows sophisticated thinking."

When students understand specifically what they're doing well, they can replicate those strategies in future work.

## The "next level" approach

Frame development areas as stepping stones rather than deficiencies. Students respond better to challenge than criticism.

**Instead of**: "Your argument lacks evidence."  
**Try**: "You've made a strong claim here. What evidence from the text could you add to make this argument even more convincing?"

This approach:
- Acknowledges their current level
- Shows confidence in their ability to improve  
- Provides a clear pathway forward

## Make it conversational

Comments work best when they feel like a dialogue rather than a monologue. Include questions that invite student thinking:

- "What made you choose this approach?"
- "How might you build on this idea in your next paragraph?"  
- "What would happen if you tried...?"

These questions encourage metacognition and help students become more independent learners.

## Create feedback loops

The most impactful feedback creates opportunities for students to respond and apply their learning:

### Quick feedback cycles
- **Exit tickets** after lessons where students can ask clarifying questions
- **Peer review** sessions using your comment criteria
- **Revision time** built into lesson sequences
- **Comment conferences** where students discuss their feedback one-on-one

### Student voice integration
Regularly ask students:
- Which comments helped most and why?
- What kind of feedback do they find most useful?
- How do they prefer to receive feedback?

## The timing factor

When you give feedback matters as much as what you say:

- **During the process** rather than only at the end
- **When students have time** to act on it  
- **In small chunks** rather than overwhelming volumes
- **Close to the learning** so connections are clear

## Quality over quantity

It's better to give focused, actionable feedback on fewer pieces of work than generic comments on everything. Consider:

- Alternating between detailed feedback and quick acknowledgment
- Focusing deeply on one aspect per assignment
- Using formative feedback during lessons to reduce summative load

When feedback feels purposeful and actionable, students engage with it. When they engage with it, they improve. And when they improve, both teacher and student experience the satisfaction that makes education rewarding.

The goal isn't perfect feedback—it's feedback that creates learning.
